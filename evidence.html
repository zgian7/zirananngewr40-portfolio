Recovered Evidence

Early pieces that still matter.

These pieces show the beginning of my WR40 growth. They are not final polished essays, but they make visible how I started moving from personal reaction into controlled description, style, annotation, and analysis.

First-Day Writing

Best/Worst Food Ever: Oreo

I think Oreo is the worst food, not because its taste, but because it shows how fragile our trust in food can be. One time, I ate Oreo and later had acute gastroenteritis. I know this was most likely a coincidence, but the experience permanently changed how I feel about it. Since then, I cannot eat Oreo without feeling uncomfortable, even though I understand logically that it was not the cause.

This experience reveals that our relationship with food is not purely rational. A single negative memory can override logic and reshape our preferences. In this sense, the "worst" food is not the one that tastes bad, but the one that becomes associated with discomfort and loss of control.

Oreo, for me, represents that moment where trust breaks. It is not just a cookie anymore, but a reminder that something as ordinary as food can suddenly feel unsafe.

Museum Label

This piece matters to me because it is honest in a very early, unfinished way. I was not trying to sound impressive yet. I was simply writing from a memory that made food feel unsafe, even though I knew logically that one Oreo could not explain everything that happened.

Looking back, I can see the beginning of my growth as a writer. The idea was already there: food is connected to trust, fear, the body, and memory. What I did not have yet was control. I could feel the meaning, but I had not learned how to organize that feeling into an argument another reader could follow.

I keep this piece in the portfolio because it reminds me that my writing often begins with discomfort. WR40 helped me stop treating that discomfort as something too personal or messy to use. It can become evidence if I slow down and ask what the feeling reveals.

Side: Description for Effect

On the Honey Chicken Super Bowl

At first glance, the bowl offers no spectacle. It does not glitter, does not threaten, does not even persuade. Its surface: grains, vegetables, a halved egg, rests in a kind of quiet arrangement, as if assembled not for display but for recognition. One could almost overlook it.

That would be a mistake.

For upon the first bite, there arrives not shock, but something rarer: a coherence. Not an assault upon the senses, but a revelation of order. The honeyed chicken does not impose its sweetness; it extends it, gently, as though aware of its own limits. Beneath it, the Bolognese answers: not in opposition, but in grounding, drawing the sweetness downward into something more durable, more earthly.

There is no domination here, no overwhelming force to surrender to. Instead, there is a quiet insistence: that nourishment need not arrive as violence, that satisfaction may be constructed rather than imposed.

Museum Label

This side feels different from the Oreo response because I was starting to play with control. I was still writing about food, but I was no longer only saying what I liked or disliked. I was trying to make the reader pause, expect something ordinary, and then feel the bowl become more meaningful.

The short sentence "That would be a mistake" is important to me because it shows a moment when I began thinking about pacing. I wanted the sentence to interrupt the description and create a small turn. That kind of turn later became important in my Food Fight essay, where I needed paragraphs to move instead of staying in one explanation.

This low-stakes piece helped me see practice writing as a place to test voice. It gave me permission to be a little dramatic, a little strange, and more intentional about mood before I had to make those choices work in a bigger assignment.

Reading and Annotating

Learning to Read Like a Writer

Annotation changed the way I understood food writing because it made me stop reading only for "what happened." Earlier in the quarter, I marked the main idea or a sentence that sounded important. Later, I started asking how the writing was working: where the voice shifted, how a scene created pressure, why a sentence felt funny or uncomfortable, and how style helped build an argument before the writer explained it.

Annotation Evidence

Bourdain: Voice, Confidence, and Observation

In my annotations on Anthony Bourdain, I noticed how his confidence comes from concrete observation. He does not only tell readers what food means; he creates a voice that makes food feel connected to danger, pleasure, attitude, and culture. His sentences feel like they already know where they are going, even when the scene is messy.

This mattered because my own Food Fight topic could easily become too careful or too nervous. Bourdain helped me see that a narrator can have attitude and still be observant. I wanted my essay to look directly at fine dining without turning into a simple complaint or sounding like I was afraid of my own opinion.

Annotating him also helped me understand why voice is evidence. The way a writer sounds can show what kind of relationship they have to food, class, pleasure, and risk.

Annotation Evidence

Kinsman: Overlooked Food and Human Feeling

In my annotations on Kat Kinsman, I focused on how she connects food to emotion and social judgment. Her writing helped me see that food description can reveal what people value, hide, defend, or feel ashamed of. That felt close to my own Food Fight topic because I was also writing about a feeling that is not easy to admit: feeling nervous in a place that is supposed to feel elegant.

This influenced my revision because I wanted my scenes to do more than describe a restaurant. I wanted small details, like menu language and nervous table behavior, to reveal pressure and belonging. Kinsman helped me trust that overlooked or awkward details can carry emotional truth.

This was one of the biggest changes in how I read: I stopped treating annotation as homework on the side of the real writing. Annotation became a place where I could borrow methods and bring them back into my own drafts.
