Reflective Introduction

Reflective Introduction

Honestly, when I first entered WR40, I did not think of myself as a writer with a clear writing system. I had ideas, emotions, and images in my mind, but I did not always know how to turn them into an essay that other people could follow. Most of the time, I wrote by instinct. I wrote the first sentence that came into my head, then followed it with the next thought. Sometimes this made my writing feel alive, because the feeling behind it was real. But more often, it also made my writing scattered. I often had something I wanted to say, but I did not have a structure that could carry the reader through it.

At the beginning of the quarter, my understanding of food writing was also very simple. I thought writing about food meant reviewing it, judging whether something tasted good or bad, and maybe describing flavor or texture. But WR40 slowly changed that idea for me. I began to see that food writing is not only about food. Food is connected to identity, culture, authenticity, class, memory, migration, and even shame. When we write about a dish, we are also writing about who we are, where we come from, what we are allowed to say, and how our tastes have been shaped by the world around us. Food is not just something sitting alone on a plate. It carries social meaning. It carries personal history. It can become a way to talk about belonging, performance, and power. This realization changed the way I approached my own writing because it showed me that the complicated feelings I have around food are not random or useless. They can become material for serious writing.

This class taught me that writing is not only about having something to say. It is also about making choices. Where should I begin? What should I leave out? Where should I slow down? Where should I show a scene instead of explaining everything? Where should I step back and tell the reader what the scene means? Before WR40, I usually treated revision as a final cleaning stage. I revised by fixing grammar, smoothing sentences, or replacing simple words with more polished ones. WR40 changed that understanding. Revision is not only repairing sentences. It is rethinking the whole piece. Sometimes the problem is not that a sentence is badly written. Sometimes the problem is that the sentence is in the wrong place, or that I started explaining before the reader had been given a scene to feel.

The first assignment that really helped me understand this was the Imitation assignment. Imitation sounds simple at first, almost like copying, but it forced me to look at the design behind a writer's style. I had to pay attention to rhythm, sentence length, repetition, transitions, imagery, and tone. I was no longer only asking, "What does this writer mean?" I was also asking, "How does this piece of writing move?" That question was important to me. It helped me realize that style is not decoration added after meaning. Style is part of how meaning happens.

In my Imitation piece, I experimented with a more poetic and intense voice. I wanted to see how language could make an argument feel emotional without becoming a traditional academic essay. I wanted the writing to press on the reader sentence by sentence. Through this assignment, I learned that imitation does not mean losing my own voice. It means borrowing another writer's structure or rhythm for a while, and then discovering what my own voice can do inside it. That mattered to me because I often feel a distance between what I can feel in Chinese and what I can express in English. The Imitation assignment helped me see that English writing does not have to be flat or only functional. It can also carry pressure, beauty, anger, humor, and strangeness.

The hardest and most meaningful assignment for me this quarter was Food Fight. At first, I did not have much confidence in it. I had many things I wanted to say about fine dining, taste, performance, and social pressure, but all of those ideas were tangled together. I knew I did not want to write a simple essay saying that fine dining is bad. That was never my real point. I also did not want to pretend that street food or casual food is automatically more honest or morally better. My real experience was more complicated than that. I was interested in the strange transformation that happens when people enter an expensive restaurant. The lighting, silverware, menu language, service, and atmosphere quietly change how people behave. I wanted to write about the tension around food, not only the food itself.

The early drafts of Food Fight showed me one of my biggest writing habits. When I really care about a topic, I tend to explain the same idea again and again from slightly different angles. I do this because I am afraid the reader will not understand me, but the result can become repetitive. Through feedback and revision, I started to understand that repeating an explanation is not the same as developing an argument. To develop an argument, I need to move the reader somewhere new. That movement can come from a sharper scene, a more specific example, a turn in the argument, or a more precise connection between personal experience and a larger issue. This became one of the most important lessons I learned in WR40.

Feedback changed my Food Fight essay in a very concrete way. It showed me that I needed to stop circling around the same point and make the essay move. During revision, I tried to make each paragraph do a different job. Some paragraphs describe the scene of the restaurant. Some analyze the social pressure behind taste. Some complicate my own argument by admitting that casual food is not automatically more real or more honest either. This helped me understand structure as a sequence of turns, not just a container for paragraphs. A strong essay does not keep proving the same point over and over. It changes the reader's understanding of that point as the essay develops.

Revision also taught me the value of scene-writing. When I first wrote about sitting in a fine dining restaurant, I wanted to explain the tension directly. Later, I realized the stronger version came from looking closely at the details: the dim lighting, the polished silverware, the menu full of words nobody wants to admit they do not understand, and the way people at the table suddenly become more careful and formal. These details allowed the reader to feel the atmosphere before I analyzed it. I learned that a scene can quietly carry an argument. It does not have to announce itself as evidence. Sometimes a fork, a menu, or a moment of silence at the table can do more work than a full paragraph of explanation.

Workshop and feedback also helped me understand that my writing process cannot stay only inside my own head. When I write, I know what I mean, so I sometimes assume the connections are already clear on the page. But readers can only follow what I actually give them. Feedback showed me where my structure became loose, where I repeated myself, and where I needed to trust the scene more. It helped me separate the idea in my mind from the essay that actually existed on the page. That separation was uncomfortable, but useful. Revision is not a punishment for bad writing. It is the place where writing becomes more honest, because the essay has to face what it is actually doing.

The smaller assignments, including Cookbooks and informal reflections, also helped me more than I expected. Low-stakes writing gave me a space to think without the pressure of making every sentence perfect. In those pieces, I could test ideas, name problems, and notice patterns in my own writing habits. I slowly realized that writing does not begin only when I open the final document. It begins earlier, in scattered notes, in questions, in failed openings, in conversations, in comments, and sometimes in frustration. Process work helped me see the invisible part of writing. It showed me that a finished essay is not one sudden product, but the result of many smaller attempts.

This quarter was honestly not very smooth for me. I was dealing with stress, moving, and moments when I felt behind or unsure of my work. The Food Fight essay especially made me nervous because I could feel that the topic mattered to me, but I did not know whether the structure was strong enough to hold it. That uncertainty became part of the writing process. I learned that I still had to revise even when I did not feel confident. Confidence does not always come before the work. Sometimes it comes after several rounds of trying, cutting, moving, and trying again.

Another important change is that I learned how to name my own writing goals more clearly. Before this class, I thought my goal was mostly to make my English sound more natural and more polished. That goal still matters to me, especially because I often begin with ideas in Chinese and then move them into English. But WR40 helped me understand that stronger writing is not only smoother grammar or prettier sentences. For me, stronger writing now means having a clearer sense of purpose. I want each paragraph to know why it exists. I want each scene to do something for the argument. I want my personal experience to become specific enough that readers who did not live through the same moment can still understand why it matters.

I also used AI during my writing process, and I want to be honest about that. I used AI for translation support, grammar checking, wording suggestions, structure brainstorming, and formatting help. Because I often begin with Chinese thoughts, AI helped me test how some ideas could move into English without losing too much of their emotional meaning. However, AI did not decide my thesis, examples, final interpretation, or personal reflection. The core experiences in this portfolio are mine: my discomfort in fine dining spaces, my uncertainty during revision, my struggle to organize complicated ideas, and my attempt to become a more active writer. AI was a support tool in my process, but I still had to decide what felt accurate, what felt honest, and what belonged in the final draft.

Looking back at this portfolio, I can see that my growth in WR40 is not only about making my final drafts better. It is about becoming more aware of writing as a process. I learned how to read like a writer, imitate with intention, revise beyond grammar, and turn personal experience into an argument with an audience in mind. I also learned that my writing is strongest when I do not flatten complicated feelings into a simple claim. The best parts of my work this quarter came from staying with complexity: liking and questioning fine dining at the same time, admiring style while noticing performance, and using personal scenes while building a larger argument.

As I leave WR40, I have a clearer understanding of what I still need to practice. I want to keep working on structure, especially how to organize ideas before they become repetitive. I also want to keep learning how to balance explanation with scene, and how to make my English writing feel precise without losing emotion. But I also feel that I have become a more active writer. I no longer see writing as something that simply happens when inspiration arrives. I see it as a process of choices: choosing an image, choosing an order, choosing a tone, choosing what the reader needs, and choosing when to revise again. This portfolio shows that process. It includes drafts, experiments, uncertainty, and final pieces that grew out of that uncertainty. More than anything, it records how I learned to take my own writing seriously, not because every sentence is perfect, but because every revision is evidence that I cared.
